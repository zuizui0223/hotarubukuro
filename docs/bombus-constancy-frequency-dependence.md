# Bumblebee flower constancy as a hypothesis for sharp local flower-colour boundaries

**Status:** manuscript-ready ecological framing, **not a new test or positive finding**. Prepared against `main` as of 2026-11-07. Retain existing frozen 67-pair calculations, figure labels, and inferential ceilings unchanged.

## Why the natural history makes the hypothesis worth testing

*Campanula punctata* has a flower architecture for which bumblebee pollination is functionally important in the studied mainland populations. Experimental *Bombus diversus* work demonstrated that wider corollas improve pollen-transfer efficiency and male reproductive success (Kobayashi et al. 1997). Field studies documented bumblebee-dominated visitation and geographical replacement among bumblebee taxa, with corresponding variation in floral size (Nagano et al. 2014). These are **pollination/morphology findings**, not evidence of a bumblebee preference for pigmented flowers. The system is valuable because the proposed agent is biologically plausible rather than a post-hoc arbitrary insect covariate.

**Regional scope:** `Bombus` is **not** the only possible effective pollinator of `C. punctata` everywhere. Inoue & Amano (1986) described `B. diversus` predominance in studied mainland populations, a mixture of `B. ardens` and halictid bees on Oshima, and halictid predominance on several other Izu Islands. Inoue (1988) reported corresponding island shifts in breeding systems. These observations motivate, but do not independently test, mainland colour evolution.

## Separate predictions; do not collapse them

**H1: focal bumblebee habitat availability (tested, weak).**
At sharp white/pigmented boundaries, higher predicted habitat support for documented widespread bumblebees `B. ardens` and `B. diversus` is hypothesized on the pigmented side. The existing 67-pair directional sign-flip test yields mean +0.03590, median -0.00277, 49.3% positive, one-sided P=0.02716 and BH q=0.08148 across three pure-transition distances; it attenuates at 10/25 km, and the raw-SDM sensitivity is null (P=0.26715). No claim of a pervasive directional pollinator effect is justified.

**H2: positive frequency dependence generated by flower constancy (untested).**
Where the colour states are perceptually distinguishable to foraging bumblebees, learned floral choice and repeated within-colour visits could confer a reproductive advantage on whichever morph is locally common. Positive frequency-dependent *per-flower effective pollen transfer*, if strong enough relative to gene flow and other forces, could drive local dominance/fixation of either state. Two neighboring patches may therefore show an abrupt white/pigmented boundary under similar measured abiotic conditions, without a consistent difference in *Bombus* SDM suitability. This is **not** a general prediction that pigmented flowers always receive more visits.

The empirical mechanism is established in laboratory bumblebee/artificial-flower systems, not for colour morphs of *C. punctata*. Smithson & Macnair (1996, 1997) documented common-morph preference and same-colour sequential foraging in rewarding artificial-flower arrays; the distinction between common-colour choice and constancy must be kept explicit. Visit sequence preferences alone cannot establish a **fitness** effect; rewards, colour discriminability, pollination efficiency and local morph frequencies can alter the direction and magnitude of selection. Other systems can show negative frequency dependence, so do not assume the sign from "bumblebee" alone.

### Simple conceptual prediction (not a fitted model)

Let `p` denote the *actual local* frequency of pigmented flowers. A stylized positive-frequency-dependence model would have `w_pigmented(p) - w_white(p)` increase as `p` increases, potentially crossing zero at an unstable interior frequency. Such dynamics favor different locally dominant states on opposite sides of a boundary. Actual stability of a spatial boundary also depends on seed/pollen dispersal, population structure and spatially varying selection. **No such frequency-fitness curve is estimated in the present dataset.**

## What existing local pairs do and do not say

- Observed 1-km flower-cell states can differ sharply over <=5 km; the fixed 67 pairs have median distance 2 km.
- Selected pairs have descriptively lower environmental distance than the general local candidate set (final eight Broad axes: 0.244 vs 0.318 median RMS). **Pairs were not chosen using an environmental caliper**, and measured similarity is not proof that conditions are the same.
- A cell with only white or only pigmented photographed flowers is **not** a census of morph frequency; it cannot demonstrate that a biological population is monomorphic or that morphs are mutually exclusive.
- Fine-scale environment, flowering phenology, genetic structure/history, restricted gene flow, microhabitat and horticultural sources remain plausible causes of sharp boundaries.
- H1's SDM association tests the direction of *predicted habitat-support differences across selected boundaries*. It **does not test H2**, whose key predictors are local morph frequency, foraging transitions, pollen transfer and morph-specific relative fitness.
- At the spatial pattern level, H2 is a **mechanistically motivated explanation consistent with a sharp boundary**, not a demonstrated driver or a statistically supported alternative to abiotic/historical models.

## Manuscript-ready English

### Introduction — natural history and hypothesis

> The floral morphology of *Campanula punctata* makes bumblebees unusually relevant candidate agents of selection: mainland populations are predominantly visited by bumblebees, and experimental studies have linked their pollination to selection on corolla dimensions. Yet their importance for floral morphology does not establish a preference for pigmentation. Bumblebee foraging also offers a different, testable possibility: flower constancy and learned preference for locally common colours could generate positive frequency-dependent pollination, reinforcing different colour states in neighbouring populations. We therefore distinguished directional correspondence with predicted focal-bumblebee habitat support from this untested behavioural mechanism.

### Methods — boundary and environment wording

> We identified pure observed white–pigmented transitions between neighbouring 1-km cells without using bumblebee predictions to select or orient pairs. We summarized similarity across the measured broad-scale environmental axes as a descriptive diagnostic; the pairs were neither environmentally matched by a prespecified caliper nor experimentally controlled. The focal analysis tested whether support for *B. ardens* and *B. diversus* was higher on the pigmented side. The available data could not estimate flower constancy or morph-frequency-dependent fitness.

### Results — no overstatement

> The 67 selected transitions had a median separation of 2.0 km and were more similar in measured broad environmental conditions than the wider set of local candidate edges. Focal bumblebee support was only weakly higher on the pigmented side in mean contrast (+0.0359; one-sided P=0.0272; q=0.0815), with a near-zero median and 49.3% of pairs in the predicted direction. The contrast attenuated over broader distance windows and was not reproduced by the raw-SDM support scale. These findings describe heterogeneous local correspondence; they do not test frequency-dependent selection.

### Discussion — cautious but biological

> Sharp changes between nearby white and pigmented flower-cell states, despite relatively similar measured environmental conditions, are not easily reduced to a single smooth abiotic gradient. An alternative, biologically grounded possibility is positive frequency-dependent pollination arising from bumblebee flower constancy: if pollinators repeatedly visit a locally common colour and this improves its per-flower pollen transfer, locally dominant colour states could become reinforced. This mechanism could produce either white- or pigmented-dominated patches, and therefore need not generate a consistent directional difference in predicted bumblebee habitat support. Nevertheless, our observations do not establish local abiotic equivalence, behaviourally distinct floral signals, colour-specific pollination, or morph-frequency-dependent fitness. Fine-scale environments, population history and restricted gene flow remain plausible explanations. Flower constancy is thus a testable interpretation of the boundary geometry, **not a mechanism demonstrated by the present SDMs**.

### Figure 3 implication

Retain the 67-pair observed-boundary/statistical evidence as the only empirical Bombus figure. Do **not** recolour the figure as a demonstration of positive frequency-dependent selection. If illustrating H2, explicitly label a small schematic `hypothetical mechanism; not measured`, separate from empirical panels and without invented transition probabilities or frequencies. ## Priority experiment

At a site where both morphs can be presented under the same environment, construct balanced and reciprocally frequency-skewed *white/pigmented* arrays (e.g. 20/80, 50/50, 80/20, **illustrative design choices only**). Track individual bumblebee visitation sequences and local offered morph frequency; estimate **visits per available flower**, conditional same-morph movement versus the frequency-expected baseline, stigma pollen deposition or paternity, seed set and effective per-flower reproductive output. Control/measure bee species, reward, flower age, display size, visible/UV spectral distance, flowering time and plant genetic identity. Test whether the *relative per-flower fitness* of each morph changes with its local frequency. Independent genetics and microclimate measurements would separate behavioural reinforcement from founder/spatial-history hypotheses.
