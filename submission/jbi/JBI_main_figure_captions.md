# Journal of Biogeography — main figure captions

## Figure 1. From hiking photographs to a two-part national flower-colour phenotype

(A) Recreational YAMAP activity photographs were converted to quantitative traits through linked date/coordinate provenance, exhaustive author validation of taxon, focal flower and petal region, duplicate/coordinate audit, deterministic sRGB-to-CIELAB conversion and optical quality control. (B) Spatial distribution of the 1,922 final phenotype observations; point fill shows the extracted visible petal colour rather than a platform colour label. (C) Response-blind four-component Gaussian mixture of CIELAB a*, collapsed across the largest gap in component means into white-like and pigmented regimes. The dashed line is the posterior 0.5 pigmentation boundary at a*=4.968780. (D) Conditional visible intensity among the 956 observations classified as pigmented; white-like a* variation is excluded rather than interpreted as low anthocyanin concentration. The measurements are based on uncalibrated display-referred sRGB and do not quantify pigment chemistry, ultraviolet contrast or pollinator-perceived colour.

## Figure 2. Broad environmental and continuous spatial structure of flower colour

(A) Posterior environmental coefficients from the final observation-level INLA-SPDE models. Pigmentation state retains the additive environmental model; pigmented-only intensity includes Temperature PC1 × temperature seasonality because this interaction improved both posterior fit and transfer to withheld geography. Its negative coefficient means that the warm-climate decline in visible intensity becomes stronger as long-term annual temperature variability increases. Topography PC1 retains a separate negative association with conditional intensity. Intervals are 95% credible intervals conditional on the structural East/West adjustment and stationary spatial field. (B) Cross-fitted natural-model pigmentation probability across 1-km cells. (C) Cross-fitted conditional-intensity prediction among cells containing pigmented observations. Panels B–C come from the separately locked cell-level natural predictive reference used for local-departure calibration and are not silently redefined by the observation-level interaction audit. (D) Residual Matérn spatial ranges and geographically blocked predictive performance. VPD/site-water-balance additions and coastline-barrier SPDEs did not improve transfer sufficiently to replace the retained models. Spatial ranges are residual-correlation scales, not dispersal distances or identified population-history boundaries.

## Figure 3. A scale shift from national geography to sharp local focal-pollinator boundaries

(A) Sixty-seven non-overlapping pure white-to-pigmented cell pairs selected within 5 km without reading Bombus values or the eventual contrast direction. (B) Selected transitions were more environmentally similar than the full set of eligible local edges in the predefined four-PC environmental space. (C) Pigmented-minus-white difference in occurrence-referenced support from the documented broad focal guild, max(*Bombus ardens*, *B. diversus*). The mean was +0.03590, but the median was -0.00277 and 49.3% of pairs were positive, showing that the nominal one-sided sign-flip result (P=0.02716; BH q=0.08148 across the 5/10/25-km pure tests) was magnitude-driven rather than a majority-of-pairs shift. (D) The directional mean attenuated at 10 and 25 km and did not reproduce on the raw cloglog scale; all-five and montane/alpine occurrence-referenced maxima were also non-significant at 5 km. Predicted habitat support is not abundance, visitation, pollen transfer or selection pressure, so the result is weak local directional consistency rather than evidence of pollinator-mediated selection.

## Figure 4. Calibrated local ecological departures and the remaining provenance question

(A) The primary event was a pigmented 1-km cell embedded among at least three cells within 10 km that were environmentally similar in the predefined four-PC space and contained only observed white flowers. Human variables and residual thresholds did not enter event definition. (B) Locations of the 17 primary field/provenance targets. Their frequency was compatible with the natural reference: candidate count P=0.19958 and candidate fraction P=0.08739 across 10,000 cross-fitted maps, with a 200,000-map joint posterior-predictive sensitivity less extreme. (C) Final candidate-minus-white-neighbour human-context contrasts using one global maxT family across population scales, DID proximity, road proximity, built-up land, forest–human edge and natural forest/mountain alternatives. Under the predeclared four-PC environmental matching, 5-km population was the leading feature but remained familywise inconclusive (directional P=0.03050; global maxT FWER P=0.20588). (D) A response-blind support-matched eight-axis environmental sensitivity replaced two of the 17 candidate identities and strengthened the 5-km population contrast; global maxT FWER was 0.04630 under the current natural model and 0.05519 under the direct eight-axis natural model. The human signal is therefore characterized as matching-sensitive short-scale settlement exposure, not a robust anthropogenic process or horticultural-provenance assignment. The sites remain priorities for field history, vouchers and population-genetic follow-up.
