# Journal of Biogeography — main figure captions

## Figure 1. From hiking photographs to two parts of flower colour

Flower-colour phenotyping of *Campanula punctata* across Japan. (a) We screened YAMAP hiking photographs, confirmed the taxon, focal flower and petal region, audited duplicate images and coordinates, and converted sRGB colour to CIELAB with one fixed pipeline. (b) The 1,922 final observations across Japan; point fill shows the extracted visible petal colour. The map uses WGS84 longitude/latitude and includes a 100-km bar scale. (c) A four-component Gaussian mixture of CIELAB a* was fitted before geography or ecological predictors were used. The dashed line marks the white/pigmented boundary at a*=4.968780. (d) Visible colour intensity among the 956 pigmented observations. The study therefore separates whether pigmentation is present from how strong the colour is once pigmentation is present. Intensity is conditional on entering the pigmented subset, and the image measurements do not quantify pigment chemistry, UV contrast or pollinator-perceived colour.

## Figure 2. Pigmentation state and conditional intensity follow different broad geography

Broad-scale environmental and spatial patterns of *Campanula punctata* across Japan. (a) Environmental coefficients from the final INLA-SPDE models. Pigmentation state was less likely toward warmer climate. Conditional colour intensity among pigmented flowers includes Temperature PC1 × temperature seasonality: at mean seasonality the temperature main effect was weak, whereas the warm-climate decline strengthened with increasing annual temperature variability. Intensity was also lower toward wetter and more rugged geography. These coefficients define thermal, water-balance and terrain hypotheses; they do not directly measure anthocyanin, flower temperature or drought adaptation. Intervals are 95% credible intervals. (b) Cross-fitted probability of pigmentation from the final eight-axis natural model. (c) Cross-fitted conditional colour intensity among cells with pigmented observations. Map panels (b) and (c) use WGS84 longitude/latitude and include a 100-km bar scale. (d) Residual spatial ranges and prediction to held-out geographical blocks. The spatial ranges describe remaining correlation after measured environment; they are not dispersal distances, genetic boundaries or proof that one response is more heritable.

## Figure 3. Boundary heterogeneity, not a uniform shift, characterizes local Bombus correspondence

Local white-pigmented boundaries of *Campanula punctata* and focal-bumblebee habitat support in mainland Japan. (a) Sixty-seven non-overlapping pure white-pigmented cell pairs within 5 km. The 5-km radius was the finest predeclared scale with enough replicated transitions and represents a local population-neighbourhood comparison, not an estimate of bumblebee foraging distance. Pairs were selected before Bombus values, environmental values or contrast direction were read. The map uses WGS84 longitude/latitude and includes a 100-km bar scale. (b) The fixed pairs were environmentally closer than ordinary local edges in the final eight-axis environment: median distance 0.244 versus 0.318. Environment did not select or weight the pairs. (c) Pigmented-minus-white difference in occurrence-referenced support for max(*Bombus ardens*, *B. diversus*). Mean support was +0.03590 higher on the pigmented side (one-sided P=0.02716), but the median was -0.00277, only 49.3% of pairs were positive and q=0.08148 across the 5/10/25-km family. A subset of large boundary contrasts therefore drove the positive mean. (d) The mean contrast weakened at 10 and 25 km and was not reproduced by raw SDM support. The result identifies heterogeneous local boundaries for direct comparison rather than a pervasive Bombus effect. The equal-elevation highland guardrail is reported in Appendix S5. The SDMs represent habitat opportunity, not visitation or selection.

## Figure 4. A distinct local-isolate event is selected, naturally calibrated and then tested for human context

Local flower-colour isolates of *Campanula punctata* across Japan. This is a node-and-neighbourhood analysis, distinct from the edge-based Bombus boundary analysis in Figure 3. (a) A local event is a pigmented 1-km focal cell with at least three neighbours within 10 km that are environmentally similar across the final eight abiotic axes and contain only observed white flowers. Human variables do not enter the event rule. (b) Applying that fixed rule to the observed map selects the **16** field/provenance targets in Japan. The map uses WGS84 longitude/latitude and includes a 100-km bar scale. (c) The same detector is replayed on 10,000 cross-fitted natural maps to calibrate event frequency. Candidate count (16; null mean 13.59; P=0.27897) and candidate fraction (0.04071; null mean 0.03107; P=0.12609) are not unusually high; replay does not identify sites that natural processes cannot reproduce. (d) Human context is examined only after the observed sites are fixed. For the post-selection null, the detector is reapplied and human contrasts are recalculated on every predictive map. Population exposure within 5 km is the strongest feature (+0.06744; directional P=0.00800), but global maxT FWER P=0.05479. The event frequency is natural-model-compatible and the human feature is familywise inconclusive; the sites therefore motivate provenance and field follow-up rather than evidence of human origin.
